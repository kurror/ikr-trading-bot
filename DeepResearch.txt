LLM-Driven Trading Systems for Live IBKR Trading
Executive summary
The main finding is blunt: as of 12 May 2026, the strongest live-trading stacks connected to Interactive Brokers are usually not “LLM-first monoliths”. The safest production pattern is a deterministic execution engine with proven IBKR connectivity, plus an external LLM sidecar for research, ranking, summarisation, and proposal generation. In that category, the clearest production-grade execution spines are QuantConnect’s LEAN and Nautech Systems’s NautilusTrader. The most coherent open-source systems that are genuinely LLM-native and IBKR-capable today are Lumibot, OpenAlice, and MMR, but only Lumibot presents a relatively complete “LLM in the loop, backtest-to-live” story without the same level of “experimental” warning language found in OpenAlice, while MMR is clearly live-capable but still owner-led and far less battle-tested publicly than LEAN or NautilusTrader. 

If you want the lowest operational risk, use LEAN or NautilusTrader as the execution/risk/reconciliation layer and keep the LLM outside the order-routing core. If you want the fewest moving parts for a native agentic workflow, Lumibot is the most practical open-source choice I found for retail or small-team live IBKR deployment. OpenAlice is architecturally interesting and rich in tooling, but its own docs explicitly tell you that live trading is unstable and opt-in only after paper validation. MMR is purpose-built for Claude-operated trading through IBKR, with a thoughtful propose/review/approve loop, but public evidence of broad production usage is still limited. 

On models, the strongest live-capable stacks skew toward proprietary tool-using frontier models rather than open weights. OpenAlice defaults to Claude Agent SDK and also supports GPT, Gemini, Codex, and several Anthropic-compatible endpoints; MMR is designed around Claude Code; Lumibot supports OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, and more through its agent runtime. In the primary sources reviewed, open-source models were either not first-class in the most mature IBKR-live stacks or were exposed through custom-compatible endpoints rather than being the default operational path. 

What was actually production-ready
For this report, “production-ready” means the system shows a credible live IBKR path with more than just order placement: restart/reconnect guidance, risk gates, order-state tracking, or execution reconciliation, plus evidence that the project is actively maintained. Profitability is a separate question and is not implied by a “Yes”. 

LEAN is the strongest evidence-backed answer if you care about operational maturity over native agentic UX. The official IB brokerage plugin is maintained separately, the docs cover local and cloud live deploys, the integration supports broad IB order types and asset classes, and QuantConnect states that it has hosted more than 200,000 live algorithms since 2015, with six months of uninterrupted uptime being common in its infrastructure. The LLM layer, however, is entirely your responsibility. 

NautilusTrader is the strongest open-source “serious trading engine” if you want to self-host and keep maximum control. Its docs are explicit about no-code-change backtest-to-live parity, startup reconciliation, persistent event recovery, dockerised gateway deployment, circuit breakers/position limits in production guidance, and a substantial IB adapter surface including contingent orders. But its own roadmap still says the project is under active development, and it explicitly keeps built-in AI/ML orchestration out of scope; you will build that yourself. 

Lumibot is the most complete “agentic strategy runtime that can also go live” among the open-source IBKR-capable tools I reviewed. Its docs repeatedly emphasise the same strategy code working in backtests and live trading, built-in AI agents, replay caching, DuckDB, external MCP tools, and an operational/deployment story that spans self-hosting and a managed layer called BotSpot. The caveat is that its IB integration surface is a bit more fragmented than LEAN or NautilusTrader: current docs show a self-hosted IB REST path, while a legacy TWS/Gateway path is still documented separately. 

OpenAlice and MMR are both worth taking seriously, but not in the same maturity bucket. OpenAlice has a real architecture, real guardrails, real IBKR support, and an impressive model/provider abstraction; however, its docs explicitly label the live stack unstable and experimental. MMR, by contrast, has an unusually sane LLM-control surface for trading: JSON-returning CLI tools, proposal state machines, auto-sizing, risk reports, bracket rollback, and a live mode. But it is much smaller, more owner-led, and does not provide the same public evidence of broad real-money usage that LEAN does.

What these systems actually do under the hood
Across the credible stacks, the architecture converges on the same pattern: LLM for judgement and orchestration; deterministic software for execution, state, and risk. LEAN does this most conservatively by leaving models entirely to the user while providing live order routing, brokerage abstraction, and data-provider precedence rules. NautilusTrader does it with even stronger emphasis on deterministic live execution and reconciliation. Lumibot, OpenAlice, and MMR move the LLM closer to the centre, but even they protect the broker boundary with explicit risk controls, approval gates, or both. 

OpenAlice’s Unified Trading Account is a useful reference design for agentic trading: AI talks to UTAs, not brokers directly; every account gets account-specific guards, snapshots, and health transitions; and orders are staged in a “Trading-as-Git” workflow before explicit push/approval. MMR lands on a similar conclusion from a different direction: the LLM produces structured proposals, reads compact portfolio/risk snapshots, and only then approves or rejects an executable plan. That is the right shape for live deployment.

Models, data feeds, and how insight is fused
The live-capable stacks in this survey do not generally ship with built-in Reddit, Discord, or alternative-data fusion as a first-class default. Instead, they centre on broker data, provider APIs, fundamentals, and news, then expose extension points for everything else. In practice, that means RSS, provider APIs, MCP servers, custom connectors, or modular data adapters. I would treat “first-class social/alt-data integration” as mostly outside the broker adapter and execution engine. That is an inference from the official docs and architecture descriptions, not a direct vendor claim. 

OpenAlice uses an in-process OpenBB engine for cross-asset market and fundamental data, plus a background RSS collector that fetches configurable feeds every ten minutes, stores them in JSONL, and exposes search/read tools over the archive. That is a strong pattern for macro/news-aware agents, but it is still feed-centric rather than social-firehose-centric. 

Lumibot’s answer is more general: it puts the agent inside the strategy lifecycle, gives it point-in-time market state through DuckDB, and lets it call external MCP tools. That is exactly how you would add SEC filings, Reddit, Discord, proprietary news, or research APIs without contaminating the execution core. Lumibot’s docs are explicit that the same agent code can run in backtest and live mode, and that replay caching can make iterative testing cheaper and more reproducible. 

MMR takes a narrower but operationally clean route: for US equities it uses Massive.com / Polygon as the primary market-data layer, including snapshots, fundamentals, and news with sentiment; for non-US markets it falls back to IB APIs. The repo even documents rough scan timings, which is unusually honest: about four seconds for a full US scan on Massive, versus roughly 30–90 seconds for international scans via IB. 

LEAN sits between these worlds. With IB brokerage, it can use IB live data, third-party providers, or both, and the docs describe provider precedence explicitly. The same engine can run backtests, optimisations, and live algorithms with no need to rewrite strategy code. Importantly, the live docs also say open history requests can take 5–10 seconds in live mode, which matters when people assume “LLM + broker API” automatically implies subsecond responsiveness. 

NautilusTrader is the most explicit about transport and operational mechanics. Its entire platform is built around modular adapters for REST or WebSocket market connectivity, with live and historical data clients separated from the execution client, and with startup reconciliation built into the live engine. For serious live trading, that separation is preferable to a single “agent that does everything”.

Two protocol notes matter operationally. First, IBKR’s Client Portal API is a localhost REST gateway with WebSocket support, browser-based authentication, and at least daily reauthentication after midnight; it is usable, but it is not a magic “cloud-native broker API” in the way many developers imagine. Second, TWS/Gateway socket connectivity remains central for many serious IB automated workflows. 

Licensing, compliance, and deployment realities
Licensing is not a footnote here. LEAN is Apache-2.0; NautilusTrader is LGPL-3.0; OpenAlice is AGPL-3.0. Lumibot’s fetched metadata was inconsistent in my sources — one part of the repo page surfaced “MIT License”, while GitHub metadata on the same page surfaced “GPL-3.0 license” — so if you are considering internal distribution, SaaS, or closed-source extensions, verify the current repository licence before proceeding. For MMR, the fetched sources did not give me a clean licence conclusion, so I treat it as unspecified here. 

IBKR deployment has its own operational rules. LEAN’s docs say IBKR LITE is not supported by that integration; you need IBKR PRO. For TWS/Gateway stacks, you must enable ActiveX/socket clients and use the correct paper/live ports. For Client Portal workflows, 2FA, single-login behaviour, local gateway constraints, and per-user market-data billing are real operational concerns. Lumibot’s docs go so far as to recommend a secondary username for automated API use, precisely because website logins and API sessions can collide. 

The compliance point that matters most is architectural: keep the model out of the direct broker pathway until you have deterministic checks around position sizing, allowed symbols, order types, and operational recovery. OpenAlice’s guard pipeline, MMR’s proposal state machine and margin/risk checks, and NautilusTrader’s reconciliation guidance all point in the same direction.

The shortest path I would actually recommend
For a new live IBKR LLM trading build, I would do this in exactly this order.

First, choose the execution spine. If you want the most mature and documented route, use LEAN. If you want the strongest self-hosted execution core with strict state/reconciliation semantics, use NautilusTrader. If you want an easier native-agent workflow and accept more retail-oriented ergonomics, use Lumibot. 

Second, separate the LLM from the execution engine. Let the model read market state, news, and portfolio snapshots, then emit a proposal object. Do not let it call IBKR order placement directly until you have weeks of stable paper results and explicit rejection logic around position limits, instrument allowlists, cool-downs, order validation, and restart handling. That recommendation is directly aligned with OpenAlice, MMR, and NautilusTrader’s operational design. 

Third, go paper first, then staged live. For TWS/Gateway-based stacks, wire paper ports first, validate subscribe-place-cancel-restart-reconcile cycles, then switch to live ports only after you have tested gateway restarts, network interruptions, and order-recovery behaviour. For Client Portal-based stacks, factor in reauthentication, secondary usernames, and user-specific market-data fees from day one. 

Fourth, pay for the data that matches the strategy horizon. If you are doing options or intraday equity work, buy at least one premium feed instead of pretending IBKR native data alone is a universal answer. In the sources here, the practical shortlist is IBKR subscriptions plus one of Polygon/Massive, ThetaData, Databento, or IQFeed depending asset class and workflow. 

Fifth, instrument and archive everything. Keep prompts, model outputs, proposals, risk checks, final orders, fills, and account snapshots. OpenAlice’s append-only event log, MMR’s event/proposal stores, and NautilusTrader’s reconciliation guidance all point the same way: the operational edge is in recoverability and auditability, not in clever prompting alone. 

Open questions and limitations
Public “used in production with real money” evidence is scarce for the LLM-native projects. LEAN has by far the strongest public operational claims; most other projects show live capability and active development, but not the same level of public deployment evidence. Exact maintainer counts were often not exposed in the fetched official pages, so I marked many as unspecified rather than guessing. Several provider-specific latency figures were also not published in the primary sources I gathered, so where transport or responsiveness was unclear, I left it unspecified rather than inventing precision.
