
LLMs in Paper Trading and Live Trading
Executive summary
Across the repositories, benchmarks, platform docs, and community discussions I reviewed, the clearest pattern is this: experienced developers and quants are not usually placing a large language model directly on the order-routing hot path. They are using LLMs upstream for research synthesis, signal extraction from text, document parsing, code generation, portfolio commentary, risk review, and occasionally as a gating layer over deterministic models and rules. The actual trading loop is usually handled by a conventional engine or broker stack such as , , , or broker APIs such as . That is an inference from the active repo set, but it is strongly supported by the way those projects are architected and by practitioner discussion stressing latency, grounding, and simulation before real capital. 

The best current LLM trading workflows are hybrid. They combine a frontier commercial model for complex reasoning, coding, and document work; a cheaper or local open-weight model for bulk classification and sentiment; retrieval or tool calls for live data; and a deterministic backtester or paper broker for validation. Public benchmark evidence also points the same way: retrieval improves textual-fundamental reasoning, but helps far less on time-series and trading-signal reasoning; programmatic or tool-assisted reasoning materially reduces numerical error compared with pure free-text prompting. 

The most robust architecture today is not “autonomous hedge fund in a box”. It is closer to a layered system: ingest data from APIs and feeds, cache and normalise it, retrieve only what is relevant, let the model produce a structured hypothesis or action proposal, score that proposal with risk and market-state checks, and then send it to a simulation or paper account before live deployment. Projects such as , , , , and all follow variants of that pattern. 

For market data, the free-versus-paid split remains decisive. Free or cheap feeds are good enough for development, sentiment experiments, overnight research, and many paper-trading systems, but they usually come with delays, partial venue coverage, symbol limits, low rate limits, or unclear commercial rights. Paid feeds matter when you need full exchange coverage, stable websockets, lower latency, institutional licensing, broad historical depth, or redistribution rights. 

If I reduce the whole landscape to one sentence, it is this: LLMs are currently strongest as a text-and-reasoning layer wrapped around traditional quant infrastructure, not as a replacement for that infrastructure. 

How practitioners are actually using LLMs
In active open-source projects, the dominant use cases are market research summarisation, financial document analysis, event and sentiment extraction, strategy ideation, strategy code generation, peer comparison, and risk commentary. explicitly models a trading firm with analyst, researcher, trader, and risk roles; fetches market and financial data and produces multi-agent equity research reports; focuses on finance-tuned models for sentiment, forecasting, and data-centric adaptation; and positions itself as a shared data layer for analysts, quants, and AI agents. 

That division of labour is visible in the text of the repos themselves. says its sentiment analyst aggregates headlines and social chatter, its trader agent composes reports into decisions, and its risk layer approves or rejects transactions before they hit a simulated exchange. describes a pipeline that fetches fundamentals, processes and forecasts them, runs specialised agents, and then generates professional reports. makes the separation even more explicit: data, strategy, backtest, then execution. 

Community discussion broadly agrees with that shape. In a recent , commenters pointed newcomers towards finance-specific tools such as FinGPT. In another recent , the most upvoted practical counterpoint was not that LLMs are useless, but that they are useful for sentiment, context fusion, and code generation when they are grounded. A separate warned that if agent consensus takes more than a few hundred milliseconds, fills deteriorate. Those threads are anecdotal, but they line up closely with the open-source architectures. 

The most important practical distinction is between paper trading and live trading. In paper trading, teams tolerate slower agent loops, richer debate, and more verbose rationales because the objective is evaluation, not fill priority. In live trading, especially intraday or venue-sensitive strategies, the LLM loop is usually narrowed to event triage, risk gating, or post-trade monitoring, while signal generation and execution remain deterministic. That conclusion is a synthesis of the repo designs, platform paper-trading docs, and community latency concerns. 

Common architectures and orchestration
Prompting, chain-of-thought, program-of-thought, and agents
The simplest production pattern is still structured prompting over fresh data: ask the model for a decision in a fixed schema such as buy / hold / sell, confidence, rationale, invalidation conditions, and required data provenance. Finance-specific benchmarks show why this is usually not enough on its own. In , retrieval materially improved reasoning over textual fundamentals, but not nearly as much for trading-signal reasoning. In the , chain-of-thought improved calculation accuracy from 60.0% to 76.9% and reduced hallucination rate versus a direct prompt, but retrieval remained the main bottleneck. 

The stronger variant is Program-of-Thought or tool-verified reasoning. found that using a reasoning model to plan and a code-capable model to generate executable Python materially improved financial numerical reasoning; the DeepSeek-R1 plus Claude 3.5 Sonnet combination reached 87.82% accuracy and corrected 91.7% of calculation errors. The same paper reports that PoT reduced token consumption while matching or beating plain CoT on harder finance maths. For trading systems, that translates into a practical rule: if a signal depends on calculations, ratios, risk budgets, or accounting transformations, push the LLM towards tools or code execution rather than trusting free-form arithmetic. 

Agent frameworks are common in research-style systems because they enforce role separation. uses specialist agents for fundamentals, sentiment, news, technicals, trading, and risk. describes a layered memory-based financial agent framework and benchmarks 13 backbone models across stocks, crypto, and ETFs. centres on profiling, memory, and decision modules to process multi-source inputs. The appeal is interpretability and modularity; the cost is latency, orchestration complexity, and non-determinism. 

Tool use and retrieval-augmented generation
The single most robust operational pattern is to keep the model out of the business of “knowing” prices, filings, and venue state from memory. Instead, use tool calls. describes function calling explicitly as a way for models to interface with external systems, while add built-in tools, function tools, and remote MCP servers. In trading, those tools are usually quote endpoints, bar history, broker balances, risk limits, filing search, news search, and portfolio analytics. 

RAG is most valuable when the source material is textual and semi-structured: 10-Ks, 10-Qs, earnings-call transcripts, macro releases, notes, and news. is built around connectors, indexing, and retrieval; its RAG docs emphasise ingestion, indexing, and metadata-rich retrieval. is especially relevant because it exposes the same data to Python, REST, Excel, and MCP agents through what it calls a “connect once, consume everywhere” data layer. That is close to the architecture experienced teams want: one data backbone, many analytical front ends.

Model choices and performance
The safest overall conclusion from public evidence is that there is no single “best trading LLM”. The right answer depends on the task. Commercial frontier models still dominate on general finance reasoning, coding, document analysis, and tool use. Open-weight models matter when privacy, cost, local deployment, throughput, or custom fine-tuning matter more. Finance-tuned specialists do best on narrower tasks such as financial sentiment or domain QA. 

For commercial models, the strongest evidence I found is mixed but consistent. The says GPT-4 and Llama 3.1 had been the best overall performers in its evaluated finance tasks, while noting that smaller models sometimes beat larger ones in forecasting accuracy and MCC. The used Claude Sonnet 4 in the strongest retrieved system it reported, and found Claude 3.5 Sonnet particularly good as the “programmer” model because it followed reasoning paths and generated precise code with less noise. In addition, a very active open repo, , currently defaults its deep and quick roles to GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-mini, which is not a benchmark result but is still a useful practitioner signal. 

For open-weight models, the picture is more nuanced. remains the reference open finance stack because it combines datasets, training recipes, sentiment models, and a forecasting demo, and it argues that lightweight, frequent adaptation is more practical than expensive monolithic retraining. In its README it contrasts its low-cost fine-tuning with BloombergGPT-scale retraining and emphasises automatic data curation. 

Two open-weight subfamilies stand out. First, general reasoning models: DeepSeek-R1-class, Llama 3.1-class, and Qwen-class models are widely supported in modern agent frameworks and are the most plausible basis for local or semi-local finance agents. Second, finance-tuned specialists: FinGPT variants and Fin-R1-style reasoning models. reports that a 7B finance-tuned model achieved state-of-the-art results on FinQA and ConvFinQA among the models it evaluated, and did so at much lower deployment cost than very large general models.

A short caution: benchmark leaders in finance QA do not automatically become good traders. shows a clear gap on trading-signal reasoning even when retrieval helps on textual fundamentals, and reports substantial gaps to human experts in reasoning and strategy coding. The most experienced teams therefore evaluate LLMs for the exact subtask they will perform, not for generic “finance intelligence”. 

Data, real-time feeds, and sentiment pipelines
How information is fetched and fused
The best active systems pull from multiple channels and keep the joins explicit. fetches company statements from Financial Modeling Prep and then lets agents produce valuation and risk views; combines fundamentals, social/news sentiment, technical indicators, and risk review; lists FMP, Yahoo Finance, and WRDS in its data layer alongside LLM sentiment preprocessing; and is designed precisely to unify proprietary, licensed, and public sources for downstream apps and agents. 

The typical production pattern is: pull raw data into a common time-stamped store; normalise identifiers and venues; annotate each item with symbol mappings and source metadata; use RAG or keyed retrieval for document-heavy sources; run bulk classifiers for sentiment and event tagging; and only then let the LLM consume the smallest relevant slice. That last step is critical because it reduces latency, context-window cost, and hallucination risk. This pipeline is partly inferential, but it is consistent with the OpenBB, FinGPT, FinRobot, and LlamaIndex designs I reviewed. 

Official APIs already cover a large share of the public-information stack: provide company submissions and XBRL facts in JSON; and EDGAR feeds help with filing alerts; expose listings and auth patterns; cover recent and full-archive post search; and let applications receive or push server events. The social APIs are not “market data”, but they are increasingly treated as event channels that enrich the state seen by LLM agents. 

Real-time and low-latency data
For real-time market data, websockets are the default. explicitly say streaming is better than polling for the latest pricing data and document websocket URLs for SIP, IEX, delayed SIP, BOATS, and overnight feeds. provide public, market, and private stream endpoints. and show the institutional equivalent. also describe streaming and REST delivery for real-time exchange data. 

Free and “developer” tiers are often enough for prototypes, nightly research, or event-driven swing systems, but they become fragile for intraday live trading. Alpaca’s own pricing page is a textbook example: the free Trading API plan gives IEX-only real-time equities, 30 websocket symbols, and 200 calls per minute, whereas Algo Trader Plus at $99 per month upgrades to all US stock exchanges, unlimited websocket symbols, and 10,000 calls per minute. That is the practical difference between “demo-grade” and “serious retail-grade”.

Social-media and sentiment pipelines
The modern sentiment pipeline is straightforward in concept and messy in implementation. You ingest posts from , , chat/server signals from , and headlines or filings from the and news providers. You normalise timestamps, authors, engagement, and symbols; deduplicate cross-posted or copied items; map text to tickers and issuers; then run fast finance sentiment models or strict LLM schemas such as bullish / neutral / bearish, event types, risk tags, and time horizon. Finally, you join those outputs to forward returns and evaluate whether the signal survives transaction costs and delay. That implementation outline is a synthesis, but it matches the repo evidence and official API shapes. 

The open-source finance stacks are already built around that idea. says its sentiment analyst aggregates headlines and social chatter into a short-term mood read. says its sentiment models are fine-tuned on news and tweet sentiment datasets, and it explicitly pitches low-cost finance adaptation as a practical advantage. 

Validation, metrics, and paper/live deployment
The evaluation stack splits into two layers: language-model evaluation and trading-system evaluation. The language-model layer includes finance-specific QA, extraction, summarisation, sentiment, and reasoning benchmarks. The uses categories such as information extraction, textual analysis, question answering, text generation, forecasting, and decision-making, with metrics including accuracy, F1, ROUGE, and MCC. That is useful for selecting models for subcomponents such as sentiment or earnings-call QA. 

The trading-system layer is stricter. is especially important because it does not stop at code that compiles; it runs model-generated strategies in a deterministic CTA-style backtesting framework with cost model and metric definitions, and evaluates execution success and deviations in financial metrics. That is closer to real quant evaluation than finance QA leaderboards. 

For document-heavy workflows, the realistic failure mode is often retrieval, not reasoning. The reports that its strongest system was much better on calculations than on factual or multi-hop questions, and its own error analysis says retrieval misses and multi-statement gaps dominate. That finding has a practical implication: before changing the LLM, check the parser, chunker, table extraction, metadata filter, and retrieval design. 

For actual simulation and paper trading, the mainstream tools are mature and usable now. describe a free real-time simulation environment where orders are filled from real-time quotes. runs live, real-time data into algorithms with fictional capital. emphasise one event-driven engine with historical-data replay and performance metrics, and its website repeatedly stresses “research-to-live parity”. explicitly warn users to start in Dry-Run before risking capital. 

The metrics practitioners care about in the trading layer are therefore more operational than “LLM accuracy”. They include cost-adjusted return, drawdown, Sharpe-like risk-adjusted performance, turnover, transaction costs, fill realism, execution success, latency, and paper-to-live drift. Some repos publish those metrics explicitly and others only imply them. Where exact metric lists were not specified in the retrieved source, I am treating them as platform-dependent rather than universal. 

A useful implementation discipline is to log three things separately: the retrieved evidence, the model’s structured proposal, and the executed action after risk filtering. That gives you an audit trail, lets you distinguish retrieval failure from model failure from execution failure, and makes it possible to calculate paper-to-live divergence. This is a best-practice inference from the systems and benchmarks reviewed rather than a verbatim requirement from one source.

Legal, ethical, and risk considerations
There are four risk buckets that matter immediately.

First, market-data and platform licensing. The and the official EDGAR APIs are public and structured, but many commercial market-data providers license redistribution very tightly. explicitly say services may not be accessed or reused for commercial purposes unless expressly stated. If a team is screen-scraping or depending on unofficial wrappers for something that might become revenue-generating, that is a legal and operational problem, not just a technical shortcut. 

Second, model reliability. Finance benchmarks repeatedly show that retrieval misses, multi-hop failures, and numerical errors remain significant. itself says performance varies with backbone models, temperature, periods, and data quality, and that trading output is not financial advice. and both carry blunt disclaimers around losses and compliance. Those warnings are not boilerplate fluff; they match the benchmark evidence. 

Third, paper-to-live divergence. and both simulate fills rather than routing to live exchanges. That is valuable and necessary, but it does not remove slippage, queue position, venue outages, throttling, corporate-action edge cases, or entitlement mismatches. In other words, a paper account validates logic and orchestration, not just profitability. 

Fourth, autonomy and governance. General-purpose frameworks such as are powerful, but the more autonomous the loop becomes, the more important audit logs, bounded tool permissions, pre-trade limits, and human override become. Financial-agent benchmarks and repo docs alike emphasise that the hard part is not merely generating an answer; it is constraining action under uncertainty.
